01A Civilization That Changed the World

Where the Western mind learned to ask questions.

In a scattered archipelago of mountains and sea, a people who never unified into an empire produced something far more durable: a way of thinking. Between the eighth and second centuries BCE, the Greek world replaced mythic explanation with reasoned argument, and replaced the rule of kings with the rule of citizens.

Greek ideas did not stay in Greece. They travelled through Alexander's conquests into Egypt and Persia, were carried to Rome, preserved in Baghdad and Byzantium, and returned to Europe to ignite the Renaissance. Almost every institution we call modern — the academy, the theatre, the senate, the laboratory, the hospital — speaks with a Greek accent.

  • PhilosophyRational inquiry into truth, ethics and being
  • DemocracyCitizen rule, debate and the rule of law
  • ScienceObservation, hypothesis and natural causation
  • MathematicsProof, geometry and the axiomatic method
  • ArtNaturalism, proportion and the human form
  • ArchitectureThe orders, the temple and the theatre
  • LiteratureEpic, lyric, tragedy and comedy
  • PoliticsConstitution, citizenship and political theory

THE AGE OF GREECE

Three thousand years, from palace citadels to the edges of India. Select a period to travel there.

Period I

2000–1100 BCE

Minoan & Mycenaean Greece

Before "Greece" existed, Bronze Age palace cultures flourished on Crete and the Greek mainland. The Minoans of Knossos built multi-storey labyrinths and painted dolphins and bulls; the Mycenaeans of the Peloponnese built cyclopean citadels and warrior kings.

  • KnossosThe great Minoan palace complex on Crete
  • Linear BThe earliest written form of Greek, used for palace accounts
  • MycenaeCitadel of Agamemnon and the Lion Gate

03The Polis

THE CITY-STATES

Greece was never one country. It was hundreds of independent poleis — each with its own government, dialect, gods and grudges.

ΑΘ

ATHENS

Philosophy, democracy, art and culture. On the Acropolis the Athenians built in marble the image of their own confidence — and invented the idea that ordinary citizens could govern themselves.

Peak
5th c. BCE
Legacy
Democracy · Drama · Philosophy
ΛΑΚ

SPARTA

Military discipline, strength and warrior culture. In the Eurotas valley Sparta traded luxury for obedience, raising an army that no Greek state could defeat on land for two centuries.

Peak
6th–4th c. BCE
Legacy
Discipline · Hoplite warfare
ΚΟΡ

CORINTH

Trade, wealth and strategic importance. Sitting on the narrow isthmus between two seas, Corinth grew rich from the traffic of the entire Mediterranean and coined the style of pottery the world copied.

Peak
7th–6th c. BCE
Legacy
Commerce · Corinthian order
ΘΗΒ

THEBES

Military power and political influence. Leader of the Boeotian League, Thebes broke Spartan supremacy at Leuctra and, for a brief moment, held the leadership of Greece in its hands.

Peak
4th c. BCE
Legacy

04Two Ways of Being Greek

ATHENS vs. SPARTA

Same language, same gods, same poetry — and two utterly opposed answers to the question of how a human being should live.

ATHENS

The city of the mind

Government
Direct democracy — every citizen could speak and vote in the Assembly
Education
Reading, writing, music, gymnastics and rhetoric
Military
Citizen navy; a formidable fleet that ruled the Aegean
Society
Open, cosmopolitan, mercantile and argumentative
Culture
Tragedy and comedy, philosophy, the Acropolis building programme
Lifestyle
Symposia, debate, art and the pleasures of the city
Values
Freedom of speech, individuality, excellence (aretē)
VS

SPARTA

The city of the spear

Government
Mixed constitution — two kings, elders, ephors and an assembly
Education
The agoge: state training in endurance, pain and obedience
Military
The unchallenged land power of Greece; full-time hoplites
Society
Closed, austere, hierarchic; citizens were soldiers first
Culture
Choral poetry and laconic wit rather than theatre and theory
Lifestyle
Common messes, no coinage, no luxury, no walls until 184 BCE
Values
Discipline, equality among citizens, courage, the collective

05The Examined Life

GREEK PHILOSOPHERS

Three men, two generations apart, who turned mythology into metaphysics and left the Western tradition its central questions.

c. 470–399 BCE

SOCRATES

He wrote nothing, owned nothing and questioned everyone. Athens condemned him to death for "corrupting the youth" and "not believing in the city's gods" — and made him the first great martyr of free thought.

Contribution The Socratic method: relentless questioning that tests whether a belief can survive definition.

c. 428–348 BCE

PLATO

Socrates' student, who turned his teacher's questions into a system. He founded the Academy in Athens — the institution that gave the word "academy" to every language that now uses it.

Contribution The Theory of Forms: that behind shifting appearances stands a permanent, intelligible reality.

384–322 BCE

ARISTOTLE

Plato's brightest student and Alexander's tutor, who brought philosophy down from the heavens into the observable world. He classified animals, wrote on drama, logic, ethics and politics — and shaped them all for two millennia.

Contribution Formal logic and systematic empirical inquiry across every branch of knowledge.

06The Pantheon

GODS OF OLYMPUS

Twelve immortals on a mountain in Thessaly — quarrelling, scheming and loving with all the excess of the human world they claimed to rule.

  • ZEUS

    Sky · Thunder · King of the Gods

    Lord of Olympus who hurls the thunderbolt and keeps the oaths. He is justice, hospitality and the order of the heavens itself.

  • ATHENA

    Wisdom · War · Craft

    Born fully armed from the head of Zeus. Patron of Athens, of weaving, of strategy and of the disciplined courage that wins without slaughter.

  • POSEIDON

    Sea · Storms · Earthquakes

    Zeus' brother, who struck the ground with his trident to raise springs and shatter ships. The Greeks feared the sea because they knew him.

  • APOLLO

    Sun · Music · Prophecy

    The archer of the silver bow and the lyre's master. He speaks through the priestess at Delphi and drives the sun across the sky each day.

  • ARTEMIS

    Hunt · Moon · Wild Places

    Apollo's twin, mistress of deer and of the untracked forest. She moves through the wilderness with her hounds, protector of young girls.

  • ARES

    War · Bloodshed · Courage

    The sheer frenzy of battle. Even among the Greeks he was distrusted — beloved by Aphrodite, hated by Athena, who represented war fought well.

  • APHRODITE

    Love · Beauty · Desire

    Born of sea foam, she binds gods and mortals alike. Homer calls her the "laughter-loving"; the Greeks knew that desire rules even Olympus.

  • HERMES

    Messengers · Travel · Trade

    Winged-sandalled herald of the gods, guide of souls and patron of thieves, merchants and lucky accidents. He moves between every world.

  • HADES

    Underworld · The Dead

    Not evil, simply unavoidable. He rules the silent kingdom beneath the earth, from which no traveller returns unchanged.

07Building in Stone

GREEK ARCHITECTURE

The Greeks built for the gods, but they designed for the human eye. Every proportion, every curve and every column was calculated to be read by a person standing on the ground.

DORIC • IONIC • CORINTHIAN

Doric

The oldest and plainest. Sturdy, fluted, with a simple cushion capital and no base. It is the architecture of the mainland — of Sparta, Olympia and the Parthenon.

Ionic

Slimmer, taller and more elegant, with a base and a capital rolled into volutes like a ram's horn. Born in Ionia, it graces the Erechtheion and its famous porch of maidens.

Corinthian

The youngest and richest: a bell capital bursting with carved acanthus leaves. Rare in Greece, it became the favourite of Rome and of every classical building since.

08Conflict

GREEK WARS

The wars that saved Greece — and then destroyed it. Hover a battle to locate it.

  1. 490 BCE

    Battle of Marathon

    Ten thousand Athenians, joined by a small Plataean force, charged across a plain of the eastern Attic coast and broke the Persian landing army. The runner Pheidippides' legend begins here.

    Persian Wars
  2. 480 BCE

    Battle of Thermopylae

    Leonidas and three hundred Spartans held a mountain pass against the army of Xerxes for three days. A defeat that became the definition of deliberate sacrifice.

    Persian Wars
  3. 480 BCE

    Battle of Salamis

    Themistocles lured the Persian fleet into the narrow strait by the island of Salamis. In confined water the Persian numbers became a liability; Greece's triremes won the war at sea.

    Persian Wars
  4. 479 BCE

    Battle of Plataea

    A Greek alliance of hoplites under the Spartan regent Pausanias finally destroyed the Persian expeditionary force on land, ending the invasion for good.

    Persian Wars
  5. 431–404 BCE

    The Peloponnesian War

    Athens and its empire against Sparta and the Peloponnesian League — twenty-seven years of plague, naval raids and siege that ended the golden age of both. Sparta won; Greece never recovered.

09Macedon

ALEXANDER THE GREAT

The conqueror who carried Greek culture across continents.

  1. 01

    Macedon

    In 336 BCE a twenty-year-old king inherited a kingdom on the northern edge of the Greek world — and an army his father Philip had forged into the finest weapon in Europe.

  2. 02

    Persia

    Crossing the Hellespont, Alexander smashed the Persian governors at the Granicus, unravelled the Gordian knot, and broke Darius III at Issus and Gaugamela. The Persian Empire fell in four years.

  3. 03

    Egypt

    Welcomed as a liberator, he was crowned pharaoh and founded the city that still bears his name — Alexandria — with its library, its lighthouse and its museum.

  4. 04

    Central Asia

    Through Bactria, Sogdiana and the Hindu Kush, across deserts and snow, fighting guerrillas and marrying princesses to bind the two worlds together.

  5. 05

    Bactria

    Greek cities were planted deep in Asia. Settlers, administrators and soldiers carried the language, theatre and gymnasium of Greece to the edge of the known world.

  6. 06

    India

    At the Hydaspes in 326 BCE he defeated King Porus — and then, at the demand of his exhausted men, turned back. He died in Babylon at thirty-two, and the Hellenistic age began.

10The Inheritance

THE LEGACY OF GREECE

Greece fell politically two thousand years ago. Its ideas never did. They are the load-bearing walls of the modern world.

  • DEMOCRACYFrom dēmos + kratos — power to the citizen.
  • PHILOSOPHYThe love of wisdom as a discipline of argument.
  • SCIENCENature explained by causes, not by caprice.
  • ARTThe human body as the measure of beauty.
  • ARCHITECTUREColumns, pediments and proportion, still copied.
  • LITERATUREEpic, tragedy and the birth of the reader.
  • MATHEMATICSEuclid's proof as the model of certainty.
  • POLITICSConstitutions, citizenship and civic duty.

Every time a parliament debates, a jury deliberates, a doctor takes an oath, a student sits an examination, a theatre dims its lights or an architect draws a column, a Greek idea is being used again. The civilization ended; the conversation it started has never stopped.
